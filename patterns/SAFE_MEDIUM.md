# `SAFE` · `MEDIUM`

*Tier two. Open this file and no other when writing a working location in a settlement region. Tier one is `DESIGN_PATTERNS.md`; the forms are in the Playbook template.*

---

## WHAT THIS CELL IS FOR

**The body of a settlement and nearly all of it.** Shops, halls, offices, wharves, temples, drinking rooms, the rows that used to be something.

A `SAFE MEDIUM` location does one of three jobs, and the best do two:

**It supplies.** Goods, hire, rest, storage, transport — at prices and with limits.
**It informs.** Rumour, record, testimony, gossip. In a module whose real reward is knowledge, this is the cell that pays it out.
**It records.** The settlement's own past, legible in what it is built out of and what it has stopped doing.

Danger is not among them. The town does not answer intrusion; it gossips about it.

---

## PATTERNS THAT POPULATE HERE

- **`OCCUPIED`** — the cell's default. Almost every location here has a person in it, and the person is the location.
- **`READING`** — ledgers, manifests, name-walls, records nobody consults.
- **`TRACE`** — a shuttered row, a dwarven course under a palisade, an economy that stopped mid-sentence.
- **`CACHE`** — attics, strongboxes, what a family kept. Always with a social consequence rather than a guard.
- **`THRESHOLD`** at working weight — a gate, a landing, a boundary between quarters.

**Absent:** `EMPTY` and `CONNECTIVE` for the reasons in `SAFE_LOW`. `STANDING HAZARD`, which does not exist in a settlement — a `SAFE` region's hazards are consequences, not threats, and they are held at the locations that cause them. `PUZZLE`, whose `SAFE` form is a negotiation and belongs under `OCCUPIED`.

## THE FORM

Three to four features. Both Overviews. **The person, where there is one, gets a feature of their own and it is usually the first.**

## THE RULES OF THIS CELL

### 1. Every location declares a service and its cost, and the cost need not be coin

**Coin** — an inn's rates, a smith's prices, a bank's percentage, a guild's dues, a mercenary's daily wage and what it includes.
**Standing** — what the old families will give to people who ask about the hold as though it had been a place where people lived, and will not sell to strangers at any price.
**Obligation** — a village that will freely give a character anything they ask for and will expect the same in future. The Referee is told to call it in at least once.
**Risk** — what carousing buys, and who remembers you afterward.

A settlement of one currency is thin. A settlement whose currencies differ by quarter is a place.

### 2. Every location declares at least one refusal

Guides who will not leave the path. Villagers who will lend canoes and will not set foot on the island. Families who will not sell heirlooms to strangers. A landlord with one door who likes knowing who is in his house.

**A `SAFE` location's refusals are as load-bearing as its services**, and in a hub they define the shape of everything around the town more precisely than any description of that ground will.

### 3. The settlement declares a ceiling

A stated cap on what the place can spend or supply. Without one, the first real hoard turns the town into an infinite bank and the region's poverty — which is usually the point — evaporates in one session.

State it at region level and let the locations inherit it.

### 4. Any find requires a stated social consequence in the same location

There is money hidden in nearly every building in a settlement: under floorboards, in a jar in a bedroom, in a crock under flour bags. This is fine — **provided the location states what happens when it is taken.**

The alarm sounds, the citizens obstruct without dying for it, the guard arrives within a turn or two and will use force, and the offender goes to the cells. Or, more usefully: theft works, and it is the single fastest way to make the whole upper town stop talking to a party.

**In `SAFE` mode the guardian is the consequence.** No consequence, no find.

### 5. Occupants carry a named relation to another occupant

The smith is chatty, likes gossip, and is not so secretly in love with the storekeeper. His sister lives with him and works at the inn. Four buildings and the town is already a closed loop of people with reasons to talk about each other.

**This is what makes a settlement runnable at the table** — the Referee does not have to invent why the smith knows what the innkeeper knows. It is also checkable: every named occupant carries at least one edge, and a region of isolates is a list of shops.

### 6. No combat statistics

Restated because this is the cell where the temptation lives. A `SAFE` location carries no hit points, armour class or damage. Personality expressed as a modifier is legitimate and better — a leader whose men have higher morale when he is with them is a trait and a number that are the same fact.

### 7. Information has a price and a reliability, and both are stated

The gate watch log nothing and remember everything, and will trade it for a drink. The wharfmaster requires that a question be worth his afternoon, and will tell a party flatly how many of the names above theirs came back down. What is said in the rough drinking hall runs harder, further and less true than the same thing said in the square — **and that is a difference a party can learn to use.**

Where an informant is unreliable, make them right once. An unreliable source telling the truth is worth more than a reliable one, because it stops the players simply discounting them.

## WORKED EXAMPLES

*Original. Set nowhere. Codes given as `X.n`.*

### `OCCUPIED` — service, cost, refusal, relation

```
### `X.7 Coyle's Ropewalk` — Two Hundred Feet of Shed, Everything Made to Order, He Will Not Be Hurried

*A long open-sided shed running two hundred feet down the slope, hemp hanging
in hanks the length of it, and a small man walking backwards at the far end
paying out yarn from a bundle at his waist.*

**Referee Overview:** An open walk two hundred feet by fifteen under a shingle
roof, wheel at the upper end, tarring shed at the lower, one way in from the
lane.

* **Coyle:** the only ropemaker within four days. Deliberate, unhurried, and
  entirely uninterested in anybody's schedule. He sells off the rack at a fair
  rate and makes to order at three days a length, and he will not shorten that
  for money, argument or emergency — the yarn takes the time it takes.
* **What He Will Not Do:** sell the tarred cable. It is spoken for by the
  charter and he has taken their coin, and he does not break an agreement to
  make a better one.
* **Who He Talks To:** the wharfmaster, daily, because every cargo needs
  lashing and every lashing gets logged. Anything Coyle hears at the landing is
  in the square by evening, and he does not think of this as gossip.
* **The Tarring Shed:** shut, hot, and the one place in the quarter where a
  conversation cannot be overheard from the lane -> `X.8`
* **The Lane:** back up to the square -> `X.2`
```

*A service with a rate, a refusal that costs the party something, a relation to a named other occupant, and a physical fact — the shed — that is useful for reasons the location does not spell out.*

### `READING` + `TRACE` — the record nobody consults

```
### `X.4 The Weighing Office` — Twenty Years on Nails, Nobody Reads Them, He Asks Your Names

*A plank office the size of a stable, hot from a stove that is always lit, and
papered floor to ceiling with paper — manifests, hung on nails in bundles by
year.*

**Referee Overview:** One room at the head of the landing, stove, desk, twenty
years of manifests on the walls in order and in date.

* **The Manifests:** every boat up the river for twenty years and every crew
  that walked off one and up the road. Cross-read against the arrivals they are
  a register of who went in — and, by subtraction, of who has not come out.
* **The Wharfmaster:** he writes names down because he has spent twenty years
  being the last person to write some of them anywhere. He trades freely with
  anyone who tells him where they are going and lets him record it.
* **What He Will Say Without Being Asked:** how many of the names above yours
  came back down. Flatly, and with no attempt to talk anybody out of anything.
* **The Yard Behind:** hard standing where cargo is checked against paper
  before it goes into the sheds -> `X.5`
* **The Landing:** -> `X.3`
```

## EXCERPTS FROM THE CORPUS

*Paraphrased. Cited so the original can be opened; not reproduced.*

**1. Cost stated as coin, comprehensively.**
*B2 The Keep on the Borderlands.* Room rates by class of bed, mercenary wages that differ depending on whether the employer buys the gear, guild dues as a percentage of merchandise, a money-changer's fee, loan interest by the month, and shops that buy back at half list. **A whole settlement economy written as rates. Rule 1's first currency.**

**2. Cost stated as obligation.**
*X1 Isle of Dread, the village of Mantru.* The villagers will freely give a character anything they ask for and will expect the same in future — and the Referee is told to use the custom at least once during the adventure. One sentence, a complete economy for a place with no coinage. **Rule 1's third currency, and the instruction to call it in is what stops it being flavour.**

**3. The guardian is the consequence.**
*B2, the Keep's standing order.* Money is hidden in almost every building — under floorboards, in a jar in a bedroom, in a crock under flour bags — and it is fine, because the module states that any criminal act raises an instant alarm, that citizens will obstruct without dying for it, that the guard arrives within a turn or two and will kill if resisted, and that offenders go to the cells. **Rule 4.**

**4. The social graph as the runnable unit.**
*Fourtower Bridge.* Every named person is defined by an edge to another: the innkeeper's husband runs the stable and says little; the smith is chatty and in love with the storekeeper; the smith's sister lives with him and works at the inn. Four buildings and the town is a closed loop. **Rule 5.**

**5. Reliability carried by the person rather than by a flag.**
*Fourtower Bridge, the fisherman.* A kooky old hooded man who sometimes sends strangers on nonsense quests — and who, on the inn's event table, claims to have caught a pike the size of a crocodile, which the module notes he actually did. **Rule 7, and the inversion is what stops the players simply discounting him.**

**6. The ambient table with a minority of hooks.**
*Fourtower Bridge, "A night at the Moorland Magpie".* Twenty entries, most with no mechanical effect at all — an apple pie, a dog with a new litter by the fire, dwarves complaining about the furniture — and a handful that carry real content: two fighters who will hire on but only as a pair, a merchant whose caravan was raided, a traveller robbed on the road, no food deliveries for weeks so stale bread is on the menu. **Those four are wilderness intelligence delivered in a common room. The ratio is the finding: ambient by default, informative at intervals.**

## FOR THE TROPE PASS

Nearly all the classic settlement furniture is `MEDIUM` and comes with its person attached: the innkeeper who knows everyone, the fence who asks no questions and pays badly, the sage with a specialism and a fee, the temple that will do the rite for anyone paid or not, the moneylender with an unpleasant enforcement arm, the retired adventurer who will not be drawn, the caravan master hiring.

Two notes for that pass. **The stock table is a reason to come back** — roll occasionally to see what has arrived, and weight it toward utility rather than power, because a settlement that sells solutions to problems is better than one that sells strength. And **the traitor in the safe house is the best `SAFE` trope in the genre**, but it only works once and only in a town whose other dealings are honest; it belongs at `HIGH`, and it is written up there.
